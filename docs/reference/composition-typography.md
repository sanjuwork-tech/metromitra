# Composition and typography

## Compose a sequence

Map the page as:

1. orientation and promise;
2. proof or mechanism;
3. offer or capability;
4. differentiation;
5. objection handling;
6. decision and action.

Not every page needs all six stages, but every section needs a clear narrative job.

## Create rhythm

Alternate intentionally between:

- dense and quiet;
- full-bleed and contained;
- image-led and type-led;
- centered and asymmetric;
- explanatory and demonstrative.

Repetition should establish a system. Variation should reveal importance.

## Go beyond the default grid

Consider:

- split-screen editorial stages;
- spatial diagrams;
- product galleries;
- layered timelines;
- pinned narrative sequences;
- oversized typographic fields;
- horizontal rails with a visible next item;
- annotated media;
- asymmetric case-study compositions;
- interactive comparisons.

Choose the structure that clarifies the content. Do not choose novelty that weakens scanning or conversion.

## Protect hierarchy

Each viewport should reveal:

- one dominant element;
- one supporting explanation;
- one next action.

Avoid equal visual weight across headings, cards, badges, and buttons. Use scale, contrast, position, and whitespace before adding borders or decoration.

## Set readable type

- Keep body measure near 45 to 75 characters.
- Use tight tracking only on display text.
- Balance display lines intentionally.
- Avoid three-line mobile headlines caused by desktop-only wording.
- Use fluid type within controlled minimum and maximum sizes.
- Preserve a visible contrast between display, lead, body, and metadata.

## Use cards only for objects

Cards are appropriate for products, plans, articles, people, files, or other discrete objects. Use rows, bands, editorial layouts, or open whitespace for narrative content and capabilities. A border around every thought destroys hierarchy.
